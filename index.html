  • Annual crossover events
  • Sequential multi-show storytelling

Crossovers as cobranding

Crossovers are not only an entertainment phenomenon; they are a distinct form of cobranding.

Traditional cobranding

  • Typically, 2-brand collaborations
  • Focus on static product-level outcomes (e.g., sales, brand adoption)
  • Effects examined during and after collaboration

Crossover cobranding

  • Multi-brand/show collaborations
  • Sequential & serialized audience engagement
  • Responses evolve dynamically over time
  • Outcomes unfold before, during, and after the event

How a crossover unfolds over a season

Research questions

Before
Do crossover events increase viewership before the event through anticipation?
During
How large is the immediate spike in the first crossover episode, and how much carries across episodes?
After
Do crossover events create post-event retention?
Moderator: relative popularityModerator: exclusivity (number of shows)
  • Prior work on traditional cobranding emphasizes the benefits of exclusivity (fewer collaborating brands). Does this also hold for crossover cobranding?
  • Less popular partners are often found to benefit more from cobranding. Do crossover events exhibit the same asymmetry?

Counterfactual simulation 1: whether to run a crossover

  • Crossover-related coefficients set to zero to construct the no-crossover baseline; compare with the observed trajectory.
  • All six events yield positive net gains: combined +6.0% to +12.5% of season viewership.
  • During-event gains grow with event scale (27% to 43%).
  • After-event gains shrink with scale (16.5% down to 2.9%): larger events spike more but retain less.
  • Largest absolute gain: Flash vs. Arrow, +19.6M viewers; the five-show Crisis on Infinite Earths, +4.9M.

Counterfactual simulation 2: which show goes first

  • Pairwise comparison: The Flash (most popular) in the first slot vs. a later slot, participating shows held fixed.
  • Flash-first raises within-event viewership in all six events, by up to +6.4% (Heroes Join Forces).
  • Post-event gains are small but consistently positive (up to +0.29%).
  • Combined gain up to +0.51% of season viewership.

Counterfactual simulation 3: when to schedule

  • Crossover moved 5 episodes earlier, or 5 and 10 episodes later, within the season.
  • Earlier placement raises total season viewership by up to +3.3%; a 10-episode delay costs up to −7.0%.
  • Gains are largest for two-show events; larger events move in the same direction with smaller magnitude.
  • Earlier placement gives anticipation and post-event retention more episodes to act on.

Theoretical contributions

01
Crossover collaborations generate dynamic audience responses that unfold before, during, and after the event.
Unlike traditional cobranding, the outcome is a trajectory, not a single product-level measure.
02
Traditional cobranding principles do not uniformly generalize to crossover media contexts.
Exclusivity and popularity exert phase-specific, and sometimes opposing, effects across pre-, within-, and post-crossover periods.
03
The findings extend cobranding theory to serialized, multi-entity media collaborations.

Managerial contributions

Before the event
  • Smaller and more exclusive crossovers generate stronger pre-event audience build-up.
  • Crossovers can strategically boost anticipation for less popular shows.
During the event
  • Launching the crossover on the most popular show increases overall audience engagement.
  • Larger crossover events create stronger immediate audience spikes.
After the event
  • Smaller and more exclusive crossovers produce stronger long-run post-event retention.
  • Post-event retention is stronger for more popular shows.

Scale, sequencing, and timing are scheduling decisions. The estimated model can be used to evaluate a crossover plan before committing to it.
